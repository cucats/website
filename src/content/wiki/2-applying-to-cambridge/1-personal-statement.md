From 2026 entry, UCAS replaced the single free-form personal statement with **three structured questions**. The total limit is still 4,000 characters including spaces, and each answer has a 350-character minimum. You can split the remaining space between the three however you like.

> [!NOTE]
> Check the exact question wording and character counts in your UCAS application, since UCAS is the authority here. Their guide is [How to write your personal statement: 2026 entry onwards](https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/writing-your-personal-statement/the-new-personal-statement-for-2026-entry).

The three questions are, in substance:

1. Why do you want to study this course or subject?
2. How have your qualifications and studies helped you prepare for it?
3. What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are those experiences useful?

The change is good news. The old format rewarded people who had been coached in how to write a smooth essay; the new one asks direct questions and mostly rewards having something to say.

## What Cambridge is reading it for

Your statement goes to the College that considers your application, and it is read alongside your grades, your TMUA score and your reference. It is not scored in isolation, and it will not rescue a weak application. What it does is two specific things:

- It gives evidence that your interest in the subject is real and that you have acted on it.
- It gives your interviewers material. Interviewers frequently open by asking about something in your statement, so everything you write is a question you are inviting.

That second point is the one to internalise. **Write nothing you would not enjoy being asked about for ten minutes.** If you mention a book, be able to discuss the argument of the book. If you mention a project, be able to explain the part that did not work.

## Question 1: why this subject

The trap here is the origin story. "I have been fascinated by computers since I was six years old" tells the reader nothing, and hundreds of applicants write it.

What works instead is being concrete about what specifically pulls you in, because Computer Science is a wide subject and the honest answers vary enormously. Someone drawn to the mathematics of what is computable is a different applicant from someone drawn to systems performance, and both are perfectly good.

Compare:

> I have always loved technology and want to understand how it works.

with:

> Trying to write a Sudoku solver taught me that my brute-force approach was hopeless, and reading about constraint propagation afterwards was the first time I understood that choosing a better algorithm beats writing faster code.

The second is shorter on enthusiasm and much longer on evidence. It also hands the interviewer an obvious and inviting question.

## Question 2: how your studies prepared you

For Cambridge Computer Science, this question is substantially about **mathematics**. The course is mathematically heavy from the first term, and demonstrating that you are comfortable with rigorous mathematical thinking is more valuable than demonstrating that you can program.

Useful things to write about:

- Mathematical topics you found genuinely interesting and why — proof, induction, discrete structures, logic, probability.
- A time your further reading connected to something on your syllabus.
- Where you struggled and what you did about it. This reads as maturity, not weakness.

Avoid narrating your timetable. The reader can already see your subjects on the form; what they cannot see is what you thought about them.

## Question 3: outside of education

This is where projects, competitions, reading, societies and jobs go — and note the second half of the question, "why are these experiences useful". The reflection is the part being marked, not the list.

Things that tend to give you something to say:

- **A personal project**, however small. What did you choose, what broke, what would you do differently? Something modest and well-understood beats something ambitious and vague.
- **Competitions**, such as the Maths Olympiad, Advent of Code, or a hackathon. Placing well is nice; describing an interesting problem you got stuck on is better.
- **Reading beyond popular science.** A textbook chapter you worked through, or lecture notes you followed, carries more weight than a famous book you can only summarise from the blurb.
- **Non-academic work.** A part-time job that taught you to manage your own time is legitimate material.

> [!TIP]
> Depth beats breadth, every time. One project explained in three sentences of real technical detail does more for you than five projects listed in a row.

## Practical advice

- **Draft it in a plain document first**, not in the UCAS form. You will rewrite it several times.
- **Write over the limit, then cut.** Cutting is what produces density.
- **Get one person who knows the subject to read it, and one who does not.** The first checks that your claims are sound; the second checks that it is readable.
- **Never let anyone else write it.** UCAS runs similarity detection, and AI-generated statements read as generic in exactly the way that costs you the interviewer's interest. It is also the one part of the application that is genuinely yours.
- **Reread it the night before your interview.** You will be asked about it, and having forgotten what you claimed is an avoidable bad start.
