All Cambridge Computer Science applicants must sit the **Test of Mathematics for University Admission (TMUA)**. It is also required for Mathematics and Economics, so it is a well-trodden test with plenty of material available.

The two things to get right immediately, before reading anything else on this page:

> [!WARNING]
> You must **register in advance** and sit the test at an authorised centre. Registration typically closes at the end of September, with the test sat in October. Your school may or may not be a centre, and it may or may not register you automatically — check yourself. Missing registration means your application cannot proceed, and there is no route around it.

Everything official lives at [esat-tmua.ac.uk](https://esat-tmua.ac.uk/about-the-tests/tmua-test/) and on the [Cambridge admissions tests page](https://www.undergraduate.study.cam.ac.uk/applying/admissions-tests). Confirm the current cycle's dates there.

## Format

|             | TMUA                                                         |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Papers      | 2                                                            |
| Questions   | 20 multiple-choice per paper                                 |
| Time        | 75 minutes per paper, 2 hours 30 minutes total               |
| Calculators | Not permitted                                                |
| Delivery    | Computer-based, at an authorised test centre                 |
| Score       | A single overall score from 1.0 to 9.0, to one decimal place |

**Paper 1 — Mathematical Thinking** tests your ability to apply mathematical knowledge in unfamiliar situations.

**Paper 2 — Mathematical Reasoning** tests your ability to construct and analyse mathematical arguments: proof, logic, necessary and sufficient conditions, and identifying where a piece of reasoning goes wrong.

Note the timing: roughly **3 minutes 45 seconds per question**. That constraint shapes everything about how you should prepare.

## How the score is used

Colleges use TMUA results to help decide who to invite to interview. There is no pass mark, but a strong score is normally needed to reach interview, and the score is then considered again alongside everything else when offers are decided.

It is one input among several rather than a gate with a fixed threshold — a very strong score will not carry a weak application, and one slightly disappointing paper will not automatically end a strong one.

## What it covers

The syllabus is deliberately narrow: broadly AS-level pure mathematics, plus a strand on logic, proof and reasoning that goes beyond most A-level courses. There is no mechanics, no statistics beyond elementary probability, and nothing from Further Maths that would disadvantage applicants whose schools do not offer it.

Read the official content specification linked from the [TMUA site](https://esat-tmua.ac.uk/about-the-tests/tmua-test/) and treat it as exhaustive — it genuinely is, and knowing what is _not_ on it saves you time.

The reasoning strand is the part most applicants have never been taught explicitly, and it is worth studying deliberately. Make sure you are fluent with:

- The difference between necessary and sufficient conditions
- Converse, inverse and contrapositive, and which of them are equivalent
- Proof by contradiction and proof by induction
- Disproof by counterexample
- Spotting the invalid step in a plausible-looking argument

## Two habits that gain marks

**Exploit the multiple choice.** You are not asked to show working, so a full solution is often wasted effort. Substituting a convenient value, checking the sign, or testing a limiting case will frequently eliminate four of five options in seconds.

For example, if a question asks for the number of real solutions of a quadratic, you do not need to solve it. For $x^2 + 4x + 5 = 0$ the discriminant is $16 - 20 = -4$, which is negative, so there are none — done in one line.

**Watch the domain.** TMUA distractors are built from the errors candidates actually make, and forgetting to check that a solution is valid is the most common of them. Consider:

$$
\log_2 x + \log_2 (x - 2) = 3
$$

Combining gives $x(x-2) = 8$, so $x^2 - 2x - 8 = 0$ and $(x-4)(x+2) = 0$, giving $x = 4$ or $x = -2$. But $x = -2$ makes both logarithms undefined, so the only solution is $x = 4$. Both values will be offered to you as options, and so will "two solutions".

The general lesson: the answer that falls out of the algebra is not always the answer to the question.

## Preparing

- **Work through the past papers.** They are free on the official site and they are the single best resource. Do them under real timing — the test is as much about pace as about mathematics.
- **Practise without a calculator now.** Mental arithmetic, surds, exponent manipulation and cancelling before multiplying all need to be automatic, and this is the thing candidates most often leave too late.
- **Review your wrong answers properly.** For each one, work out whether you misread, made an arithmetic slip, or had a genuine gap. The three need completely different fixes.
- **STEP and Maths Olympiad questions** are useful supplementary practice for the "unfamiliar problem" reflex, even though neither is required for this course.

## The CSAT

If you apply to **Peterhouse or Trinity**, you will additionally need to sit the **Computer Sciences Aptitude Test (CSAT)**. Requirements and arrangements are set by those Colleges, so check with them directly and factor it into your College choice.

## Sitting the test

Both papers are on the same day. Get to the centre early, take permitted ID, and expect the on-screen interface to feel slightly unfamiliar — the official practice materials use the same platform, so use them at least once so that the format is not a surprise.

There is no negative marking. Never leave a question blank.
