From 2026 entry, UCAS replaced the free-form personal statement with three questions. The overall limit is still 4,000 characters including spaces, with a 350-character minimum on each answer, and you can split the remaining space however you like.

> [!NOTE]
> UCAS is the authority on the exact wording and counts, and both appear in your application. Their guide is [How to write your personal statement: 2026 entry onwards](https://www.ucas.com/applying/applying-to-university/writing-your-personal-statement/the-new-personal-statement-for-2026-entry).

In substance, the three questions are:

1. Why do you want to study this course or subject?
2. How have your qualifications and studies helped you prepare for it?
3. What else have you done to prepare outside of education, and why are those experiences useful?

This is an improvement. The old format rewarded people who had been coached to write a smooth essay. The new one asks direct questions, and what it mostly rewards is having something to say.

## What Cambridge does with it

Your statement goes to the College considering your application, and gets read alongside your grades, your TMUA score and your reference. It will not rescue a weak application, and it is not scored on its own. It does two specific things.

It gives evidence that your interest is real and that you have acted on it. And it gives your interviewers material, because they routinely open by asking about something in it.

That second point is the one to absorb. Write nothing you would not enjoy discussing for ten minutes. Every book you name is a question you have invited.

## Question one: why this subject

The trap is the origin story. "I have been fascinated by computers since I was six" tells the reader nothing and hundreds of applicants write it.

Being concrete works better, because Computer Science is wide and the honest answers vary. Someone drawn to what is computable is a different applicant from someone drawn to systems performance, and both are perfectly good.

Compare:

> I have always loved technology and want to understand how it works.

with:

> Writing a Sudoku solver taught me that my brute-force approach was hopeless, and reading about constraint propagation afterwards was the first time I understood that a better algorithm beats faster code.

The second has less enthusiasm and much more evidence. It also hands an interviewer an obvious question, which is the point.

## Question two: how your studies prepared you

For Cambridge Computer Science this question is largely about **mathematics**. The course is mathematically heavy from the first term, and showing you are comfortable with rigorous mathematical thinking counts for more than showing you can program.

Things worth writing about: mathematical topics you found interesting and why, particularly proof, induction, discrete structures, logic and probability; a time your reading connected to something on your syllabus; and where you struggled and what you did about it, which reads as maturity.

Avoid narrating your timetable. Your subjects are already on the form. What is not on the form is what you thought about them.

## Question three: outside of education

Projects, competitions, reading, societies and jobs go here. Note the second half of the question, "why are these experiences useful". The reflection is the part being assessed, and a list on its own does nothing.

A personal project of any size gives you something to say. What did you choose, what broke, what would you do differently? Something modest that you understand beats something ambitious you can only describe vaguely.

Competitions work well, whether that is the Maths Olympiad, Advent of Code or a hackathon. Placing highly is nice. Describing an interesting problem you got stuck on is better.

Reading counts most when it goes past popular science. A textbook chapter you worked through carries more weight than a famous book you can summarise from the blurb. Non-academic work is legitimate material too; a part-time job that taught you to manage your own time is worth a sentence.

> [!TIP]
> Depth beats breadth every time. One project explained in three sentences of real technical detail does more for you than five listed in a row.

## Practical advice

Draft it somewhere other than the UCAS form, because you will rewrite it several times. Write over the limit and then cut, since cutting is what produces density.

Get two people to read it: one who knows the subject, to check your claims hold up, and one who does not, to check it reads.

Write it yourself. UCAS runs similarity detection, and AI-generated statements read as generic in exactly the way that loses an interviewer's attention. It is also the one part of the application that is actually yours.

Reread it the night before your interview. Having forgotten what you claimed is an avoidable way to start badly.
